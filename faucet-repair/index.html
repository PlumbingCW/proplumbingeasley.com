Why faucets leak

Faucets have moving parts that wear. Compression faucets use washers that harden. Cartridge and ball faucets use seals and springs that fatigue. A drip from the spout, a leak at the base and a wet cabinet each point to a different part.

A single drip can waste several gallons a day, which shows up on the meter over time. If your bill jumped, a dripping faucet is worth ruling out along with other leaks, which our leak detection team can trace.

When a faucet or valve is an emergency

Stop the flow first

.

A valve that will not close or a supply line that has split can flood a cabinet in minutes.

  1. Turn the small shutoff valve under the sink clockwise to close it.
  2. If that valve is stuck or leaking, close the main water shutoff.
  3. Open another faucet at a lower level to relieve pressure.
  4. Empty the cabinet and mop the floor to limit damage.
  5. Call the 24/7 line and tell us what failed.

Old shutoff valves often seize. If you cannot close one, do not force it. We replace it with a quarter-turn valve.

Shutoff valves and supply lines

The valve under a sink or behind a toilet is the one you will need in a leak. These often go unused for decades and then fail when turned. A braided supply line that looks fine can corrode inside its nut.

We replace worn valves and supply lines during a faucet repair so the next emergency is a quick turn, not a call at midnight.

Bathtub and shower faucets

Tub spouts and shower valves have their own parts. A shower that will not shut off completely often needs a new cartridge. Behind-the-wall valves may need access panels.

If a tub faucet leaks or a diverter fails, we diagnose before opening anything and tell you what access we need.

Repair or replace the faucet

If the faucet is a name brand with available parts, repair is usually cheaper. If it is corroded, discontinued or part of a worn fixture, replacement makes sense. We can install faucets you provide or help choose one, see plumbing installation for related work.

Why a drip is worth fixing

A faucet that drips once per second wastes thousands of gallons over a year. On hot lines, it also wastes the energy used to heat the water. Most drips come from a worn part, and the part is cheap. The labor is a short visit.

A drip also tells you something about the faucet. If the repair uses a part that is hard to find, or the faucet body is corroded, replacing the faucet may be the better move. We tell you which one makes sense when we see it.

Standards that cover faucets and supply lines

Faucets and supply connections sold for drinking water are tested to NSF/ANSI 61 for health effects, and the maximum flow of most lavatory faucets is set by federal rules. We use listed parts so a repair does not void the faucet's rating.
