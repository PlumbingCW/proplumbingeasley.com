Is tankless right for your house?

Tankless heaters suit households that want continuous hot water and wall space instead of a tank. They struggle when too many fixtures run at once, because each unit has a flow limit.

We size the unit to your peak demand, not your average. If it makes more sense to stay with a tank, we will say so, and you can compare on our installation page.

Common reasons tankless units stop working

Error codes vary by brand, but the underlying causes repeat.

  • Mineral buildup on the heat exchanger, which restricts flow and triggers overheating faults.
  • A clogged inlet filter or a closed isolation valve.
  • Low gas pressure or an undersized gas line.
  • Blocked vent or condensate drain on high-efficiency models.
  • A failed flame sensor, igniter or flow sensor.

Flush and descale: what it is and when

A flush circulates a cleaning solution or plain water through the heat exchanger using isolation valves and a pump. It clears scale before it hardens and shortens the unit's life. Manufacturers usually recommend a flush about once a year, and more often where the water is hard.

Easley Combined Utilities reported average hardness of 6 mg/L in 2023, which is very soft. Scale builds slowly here, so many owners can follow the manufacturer's standard schedule and check the inlet filter more often than the exchanger.

Gas supply and venting: the hidden cost

A tankless gas heater often draws more gas at once than the tank it replaces. If the existing line is too small, the unit can shut down at high demand. We size the pipe from the meter or regulator to the heater before we quote.

Venting also changes. Many tankless models use special vent materials and a condensate drain, so the old flue may not be reusable.

Repair or replace a tankless unit

Tankless units can last 20 years or more with care, so a repair is usually worth it. If the heat exchanger has failed or the unit is badly scaled and out of warranty, replacement may cost less than a long list of parts.

For leaks, see water heater repair for what to shut off first. Planned swaps are quoted on the installation page.

Questions to ask before you switch to tankless

These questions keep the project from surprising you.

  • What flow do I need at winter water temperature?
  • Does my gas line and meter support a tankless unit?
  • How will the unit vent, and where will it exit the house?
  • Where will the condensate drain, if the unit has one?
  • How often should the unit be flushed, and are service valves included?

Why a tankless unit can disappoint

The most common complaint is not a defective unit. It is a unit that was sized for average use and then asked to feed three outlets at once. The cure is sizing by peak demand and, in a large house, using two units.

The second complaint is a cold sandwich, a short burst of cold water between two hot draws. That comes from the unit shutting off and restarting. A recirculation setup or a small buffer tank can reduce it. We explain the trade-offs before you buy so the house works the way you expect.

Standards for tankless gas and electric units

Gas tankless units are certified to ANSI Z21.10.3, and the manufacturer's manual sets the vent material, clearances and condensate drain rules. We follow the manual first and the plumbing code second, and we flag any difference before we quote.
