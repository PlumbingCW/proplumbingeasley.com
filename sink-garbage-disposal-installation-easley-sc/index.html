Before you buy a disposal

A disposal is not the right answer for every kitchen. It needs a sink drain it can mount to, a power source and a drain line that can handle the added solids. Some homes on septic systems avoid disposals because ground food adds load to the tank.

Horsepower matters too. A half-horsepower unit handles light use, while three-quarters or one horsepower grinds tougher scraps and runs quieter. A continuous-feed unit starts with a wall switch, and a batch-feed unit runs when the lid is turned.

Steps in a typical installation

The job is simple when everything is ready.

  1. Switch off power at the breaker and disconnect the old unit, if any.
  2. Remove the sink flange and clean the opening.
  3. Mount the new flange and snap ring on the sink.
  4. Connect the electrical supply and install the unit on the mounting ring.
  5. Connect the drain trap and, if present, the dishwasher hose.
  6. Knock out the dishwasher plug if a dishwasher is connected.
  7. Run water and test for leaks and noise.

The dishwasher knockout

New disposals ship with a plug in the dishwasher inlet. If the dishwasher drains through the disposal and the plug stays in, the dishwasher cannot drain. That is a classic mistake after a DIY install. We check the dishwasher drain every time.

Many codes also call for a high loop or an air gap on the dishwasher drain so wastewater cannot flow back. We set that up to the manufacturer's directions.

Habits that keep a disposal working

Run cold water before, during and after use. Feed small amounts at a time. Skip grease, coffee grounds in bulk, stringy vegetables, bones and starchy foods like rice and pasta in large amounts. Those go down the pipe and then settle in the line.

If a unit jams, switch it off and use the hex wrench in the bottom to free it. If it hums but does not turn, or leaks from the body, see garbage disposal repair. A repeated slow kitchen drain may be a line problem, covered under drain cleaning.

Noise, size and under-sink space

Before you order, measure the space under the sink. Larger units are longer, and a deep cabinet with a trap, a dishwasher hose and a filter tank under it can leave little room. A unit that fits on paper may not clear the trap arm in practice.

Noise is the other trade-off. Models with more insulation and a heavier motor run quieter, and units with a larger grinding chamber handle more at once. If your kitchen is open to a living room, the quieter model is worth the price.

If you have a double bowl sink, the disposal usually mounts on one side, and the other side runs to the trap with a tailpiece. We check the sink opening and the plumbing layout before we quote.

What not to put down a disposal

A new unit lasts longer when you keep these out.

  • Grease, fat and oil, which coat the drain line.
  • Fibrous scraps such as corn husks, celery and artichoke leaves in bulk.
  • Large amounts of rice, pasta or potato peels, which swell into paste.
  • Bones, fruit pits and shells, which can jam the grinders.
  • Coffee grounds in large amounts, which pack into a sludge in the trap.

The code point most DIY installs miss

The International Plumbing Code (IPC) calls for an air gap or a high loop on a dishwasher drain, and the manufacturer's instructions require removing the knockout plug when a dishwasher connects to the disposal.

What this means in Easley, SC

Many homes in the Easley area use city sewer, and some rural properties outside town use septic. Ask which you have before you buy. If you are on a septic system, ask the septic service whether a disposal is a good idea for your tank size.

City sewer lines in older parts of Easley can carry roots and grease buildup, so a disposal should be used with care there. Easley water is soft, which makes appliances and fixtures easier to maintain. Ask the City of Easley building department about permit rules for any electrical change at your address.
